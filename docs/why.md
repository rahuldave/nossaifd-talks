# Why the rules

The sources of [the talk guide](../TALKS.md) do not always agree. This page lists each disagreement about
talks and slides, what each source says, and what the talk guide chose. [Sources and credit](sources.md)
says who wrote each source.

Most disagreements go away when you ask how a person takes in the text. There are three ways:

- **Reading** a long document in order, such as a report or a README. The reader can go back.
- **Skimming** many short items, such as titles, list items, notifications or the rows of a dashboard.
  The reader sees the first two words of each item.
- **Listening** to a talk. The listener hears each sentence once. Rosalind Reid says that the listener sees
  each slide for less than a minute
  ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 32](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)).

A rule that is right for one way can be wrong for another. So each choice below names the way it is for.

## Two principles

The project's author set these two principles. They decide most of the choices below.

1. **Rosalind Reid's advice comes before Shankar's.** When the two disagree about the same kind of text,
   the guides follow Rosalind Reid. She edited *American Scientist* from 1992 to 2008, and she taught her
   tutorial at Harvard. Shankar's advice stays where Rosalind Reid gives none, such as for status notes,
   pull requests and short items read in bulk.
2. **A rule can give way to the story.** A writer can break a rule when the narrative needs it. For example,
   several questions in a row can build tension, and a story about a bug hunt can hold back the cause until
   the end. Think hard before you break a rule, and break it only for the story. Most rules of the talk
   guide are preferences, and a few are hard limits.

## Choices that follow Rosalind Reid

| Question | What the sources say | The choice |
|---|---|---|
| Analogy, metaphor and humor | Rosalind Reid recommends them for written, visual and spoken work ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 40](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). Shankar's skill forbids analogy and metaphor ([rule 21](https://github.com/docwriter-org/plain-writing-skill/blob/f0d3630983ac7a82aa580f1c1509d72df739ee12/skills/plain-writing/SKILL.md?plain=1#L222)) | Use them on slides and in the script, when they explain the work |
| The structure of slide titles | Rosalind Reid mixes questions and phrases in her titles ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, pages 13, 19 and 25](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). Shankar's post asks for one structure in every item ([principle 3](https://fsdatalab.github.io/blog/agent-text-at-scale/#3-use-the-same-structure-and-the-same-vocabulary)) | Slide titles can mix questions, phrases and sentences. One structure stays the rule for short items read in bulk, where Rosalind Reid gives no advice |
| Questions in a talk | Rosalind Reid uses questions as slide titles ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 13](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)), and her presentation tips ask two in a row ([Rosalind Reid, presentation tips, page 4](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-presentation-tips.pdf)). Shankar's skill forbids several questions in a row ([rule 23](https://github.com/docwriter-org/plain-writing-skill/blob/f0d3630983ac7a82aa580f1c1509d72df739ee12/skills/plain-writing/SKILL.md?plain=1#L240-L246)) | A slide title can be a question. Prefer one question and its answer. Several questions in a row are allowed when they build tension |
| Items on a slide | Rosalind Reid gives 3 to 9 items as the limit of short-term memory ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 27](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). The guide for reports keeps a list to about four items | Rosalind Reid's limit: at most 9 items on a slide |

## Choices of the project's author

In these choices, Rosalind Reid's advice and Shankar's advice do not conflict, or no source gives an
answer.

| Question | What the sources say | The choice |
|---|---|---|
| Where does the new fact go in a sentence? | Rosalind Reid puts it last in a written sentence and first in a spoken one ([Rosalind Reid, written reports, pages 5 to 7](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-written-reports.pdf)). Shankar's post puts the words that set an item apart first ([principle 2](https://fsdatalab.github.io/blog/agent-text-at-scale/#2-put-the-most-important-words-first)) | Reading: last. Listening: first. Skimming: the words that set the item apart go first |
| Contractions | Shankar's skill allows them. The guide for reports forbids them for readers who are not native speakers | Slides: no. A spoken script: yes |
| The length of a spoken sentence | No source gives a number for speech | Usually 20 to 25 words at most, as in writing. An occasional longer sentence is fine when its punctuation makes it clear |
| Speaking time | Rosalind Reid says a viewer sees a slide for less than a minute ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 32](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). No source gives a pace or a time to speak | The talk guide does not check speaking time or pace |
| Where the thanks go | Rosalind Reid's deck thanks the team and the audience at the end ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 39](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). Her presentation tips say a talk often starts with thanks ([Rosalind Reid, presentation tips, page 2](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-presentation-tips.pdf)) | Brief thanks to the hosts at the start. The team, the funders and the audience at the end |
| Numbers that no source gives | The talk checker needs exact numbers | More than 40 words on a slide is a hard problem, and so are more than 2 equations |
| Time for the approach | Rosalind Reid calls the approach the most important part ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 25](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). No source gives it a share of the time | Give the approach the most care. No share of time |

## Choices for documents

The guide for reports makes its own choices. Examples are the passive voice, where the result goes and
short items read in bulk. The page [Why the rules](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd/blob/main/docs/why.md) of
nossaifd gives them.
