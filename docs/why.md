# Why the rules

The sources of [the talk guide](../TALKS.md) do not always agree. This page lists each disagreement about
talks and slides, what each source says, and what the talk guide chose. [Sources and credit](sources.md)
says who wrote each source.

Most disagreements go away when you ask how a person takes in the text. There are three ways:

- **Reading** a long document in order, such as a report or a README. The reader can go back.
- **Skimming** many short items, such as titles, list items, notifications or the rows of a dashboard.
  The reader sees the first two words of each item.
- **Listening** to a talk. The listener hears each sentence once. Rosalind Reid says that the listener sees
  each slide for less than a minute
  ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 32](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)).

A rule that is right for one way can be wrong for another. So each choice below names the way it is for.

## Rosalind Reid before Shankar, and the story before a rule

The project's author set these two principles. Most of the choices below follow from them.

1. **Rosalind Reid's advice comes before Shankar's.** When the two disagree about the same kind of text,
   the guides follow Rosalind Reid. She edited *American Scientist* from 1992 to 2008, and she taught her
   tutorial at Harvard. Shankar's advice stays where Rosalind Reid gives none, such as for status notes,
   pull requests and short items read in bulk. Jonathan Schwabish's book *Better Presentations* comes after
   Rosalind Reid too. His advice fills the gaps in hers, such as the plan of a talk, and the table below
   gives each place where the two disagree.
2. **A rule can give way to the story.** A writer can break a rule when the narrative needs it. For example,
   several questions in a row can build tension, and a story about a bug hunt can hold back the cause until
   the end. Think hard before you break a rule, and break it only for the story. Most rules of the talk
   guide are preferences, and a few are hard limits.

## Choices that follow Rosalind Reid

| Question | What the sources say | The choice |
|---|---|---|
| Analogy, metaphor and humor | Rosalind Reid recommends them for written, visual and spoken work ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 40](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). Shankar's skill forbids analogy and metaphor ([rule 21](https://github.com/docwriter-org/plain-writing-skill/blob/f0d3630983ac7a82aa580f1c1509d72df739ee12/skills/plain-writing/SKILL.md?plain=1#L222)) | Use them on slides and in the script, when they explain the work |
| The structure of slide titles | Rosalind Reid mixes questions and phrases in her titles ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, pages 13, 19 and 25](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). Shankar's post asks for one structure in every item ([principle 3](https://fsdatalab.github.io/blog/agent-text-at-scale/#3-use-the-same-structure-and-the-same-vocabulary)) | Slide titles can mix questions, phrases and sentences, and a sentence that states the point is preferred, by the table below. One structure stays the rule for short items read in bulk, where Rosalind Reid gives no advice |
| Questions in a talk | Rosalind Reid uses questions as slide titles ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 13](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)), and her presentation tips ask two in a row ([Rosalind Reid, presentation tips, page 4](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-presentation-tips.pdf)). Shankar's skill forbids several questions in a row ([rule 23](https://github.com/docwriter-org/plain-writing-skill/blob/f0d3630983ac7a82aa580f1c1509d72df739ee12/skills/plain-writing/SKILL.md?plain=1#L240-L246)) | A slide title can be a question. Prefer one question and its answer. Several questions in a row are allowed when they build tension |
| Items on a slide | Rosalind Reid gives 3 to 9 items as the limit of short-term memory ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 27](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). The guide for reports keeps a list to about four items | Rosalind Reid's limit: at most 9 items on a slide |

## Choices of the project's author

In these choices, Rosalind Reid's advice and Shankar's advice do not conflict, or no source gives an
answer.

| Question | What the sources say | The choice |
|---|---|---|
| Where does the new fact go in a sentence? | Rosalind Reid puts it last in a written sentence and first in a spoken one ([Rosalind Reid, written reports, pages 5 to 7](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-written-reports.pdf)). Shankar's post puts the words that set an item apart first ([principle 2](https://fsdatalab.github.io/blog/agent-text-at-scale/#2-put-the-most-important-words-first)) | Reading: last. Listening: first. Skimming: the words that set the item apart go first |
| Contractions | Shankar's skill allows them. The guide for reports forbids them for readers who are not native speakers | Slides: no. A spoken script: yes |
| The length of a spoken sentence | No source gives a number for speech | Usually 20 to 25 words at most, as in writing. An occasional longer sentence is fine when its punctuation makes it clear |
| Speaking time | Rosalind Reid says a viewer sees a slide for less than a minute ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 32](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). No source gives a pace or a time to speak | The talk guide does not check pace. It plans at least a minute of the talk for each slide, by the table below |
| Numbers that no source gives | The talk checker needs exact numbers | More than 40 words on a slide is a hard problem, and so are more than 2 display equations |
| Time for the approach | Rosalind Reid calls the approach the most important part ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 25](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)). No source gives it a share of the time | Give the approach the most care. No share of time |

## Choices between Rosalind Reid and Schwabish

Schwabish writes for "scholars, researchers, and wonks", who sell a result. Rosalind Reid writes for students
who tell the story of a project. Rosalind Reid comes first. The project's author settled each choice below.
Schwabish's pages are those printed in his book, which is not online.

| Question | What the sources say | The choice |
|---|---|---|
| The shape of a talk | Schwabish's hourglass gives the conclusion at the start and again at the end (pages 11 to 14). Rosalind Reid's story holds the result for the end ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 16](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)) | The asymmetric hourglass is the default. It opens with the result at a level that holds back its detail, and the detail comes at the end. A call to action uses Schwabish's symmetric hourglass. Only a pure story uses the funnel, which builds to the result |
| Thanks at the start | Schwabish says not to open with "Thank you for having me" (page 18). Rosalind Reid's presentation tips thank the hosts at the start ([Rosalind Reid, presentation tips, page 2](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-presentation-tips.pdf)) | Rosalind Reid's brief thanks, then the opening statement. Thank the audience again at the end |
| The last slide | Schwabish says a "Thank You!" or "Questions?" slide wastes the moment (pages 141 and 142). Rosalind Reid's deck ends with thanks to the team, then to the audience ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 39](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)) | The team and the funders on the slide after the title slide. The last slide shows the conclusion, with "Questions?" under it. The thanks are spoken |
| A mixed audience | Schwabish aims the message at the group that matters most (page 16). Rosalind Reid makes each slide work for the listener with the least background ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, page 30](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)) | Both. The message aims at the group that matters most, and each slide stays clear to the listener with the least background |
| Slide titles | Schwabish asks for an active headline that states the point (page 70). Rosalind Reid mixes questions and phrases ([Rosalind Reid, tutorial deck, pages 13, 19 and 25](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-2015-communicating-your-work-deck.pdf)) | Prefer a sentence that states the point. A question or a phrase is fine when the story needs it |
| How many slides | Schwabish uses slides freely, and he rejects one slide for each minute (page 74). Rosalind Reid lists too many slides as a common problem ([Rosalind Reid, presentation tips, page 2](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-presentation-tips.pdf)) | Plan at least a minute for each slide, also for a slide that builds one point at a time. Fewer slides is better. The checker notes more slides than 75 percent of the minutes, the time that rule 87 plans for |
| The script | Schwabish says not to memorize or read a talk, and to keep the notes short (pages 20 and 158). Rosalind Reid writes out everything she wants to say first ([Rosalind Reid, presentation tips, page 5](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-presentation-tips.pdf)) | The script is in the notes, for practice, and it can be loose. A line of cues starts the notes, and it is clearly marked |
| Quotes from the book | The book is under copyright, and no permission covers it | The worksheet, its section and his examples of openings and closings are quoted under fair use, with the pages. The rest is in this guide's own words |

## Choices about the declaude skill

GUIDE.md of nossaifd took rules 50 to 55 from [Oskar Austegard's declaude skill](https://github.com/oaustegard/claude-skills/tree/6baa7290da972191749acda03c63297e67b3fc26/declauding).
It also changed rules 9, 10, 20, 32, 34, 36, 37, 38 and 43 with parts of the skill. The project's author chose on 10
November 2026 to apply them to talks, with four exceptions for the script. The exception to rule 50 follows
Rosalind Reid's advice to repeat a key idea. The other three are the project's own, for speech.

Oskar Austegard calls the skill declauding. nossaifd calls its copy declaude, because check.py and the linter read the word declauding as an -ing form.

| Rule of GUIDE.md | What it asks | The choice for a talk |
|---|---|---|
| 50 | No short, quotable line at the end of a paragraph that repeats it ([Oskar Austegard's declaude skill, register entry 12, "Aphoristic closer"](https://github.com/oaustegard/claude-skills/blob/6baa7290da972191749acda03c63297e67b3fc26/declauding/references/register.md?plain=1#L234)) | Slides and the plan: yes. The script: a line that repeats the point is correct, because Rosalind Reid asks a speaker to repeat a key idea ([Rosalind Reid, presentation tips, page 2](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/sources/reid/reid-presentation-tips.pdf)) |
| 36 | An answer to a question in full sentences ([Oskar Austegard's declaude skill, register entry 10](https://github.com/oaustegard/claude-skills/blob/6baa7290da972191749acda03c63297e67b3fc26/declauding/references/register.md?plain=1#L201)) | Slides: yes. The script: a short phrase can answer, as in speech, by rule 35 of the talk guide |
| 37 | A noun in place of a vague "This" ([Oskar Austegard's declaude skill, register entry 40](https://github.com/oaustegard/claude-skills/blob/6baa7290da972191749acda03c63297e67b3fc26/declauding/references/register.md?plain=1#L788)) | Slides: yes. The script: "this" can point at what is on the screen |
| 38 | A count only in front of a list that says what the items share ([Oskar Austegard's declaude skill, register entry 38](https://github.com/oaustegard/claude-skills/blob/6baa7290da972191749acda03c63297e67b3fc26/declauding/references/register.md?plain=1#L720)) | Slides: yes. The script: a count that says what the items share is correct with no list, because the listener cannot see one |
| 20 | No teaser that promises an answer and names nothing ([Oskar Austegard's declaude skill, register entries 8](https://github.com/oaustegard/claude-skills/blob/6baa7290da972191749acda03c63297e67b3fc26/declauding/references/register.md?plain=1#L175) and [42](https://github.com/oaustegard/claude-skills/blob/6baa7290da972191749acda03c63297e67b3fc26/declauding/references/register.md?plain=1#L843)) | Rule 17 of the talk guide states the problem of a funnel as a puzzle with facts |
| 9, 10, 34, 51 to 55 | The rest | Apply as they are. Apply rule 10, not more than two nouns in a row, with care in a title |

The talk checker does not measure these rules. `just declaude` runs the linter of
[Oskar Austegard's declaude skill](https://github.com/oaustegard/claude-skills/tree/6baa7290da972191749acda03c63297e67b3fc26/declauding) on the script, and its notes never fail the check.

The project's author chose on 10 November 2026 to revise the script in the order of the passes of
nossaifd. The order is in [docs/declaude.md of nossaifd](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd/blob/main/docs/declaude.md).
First comes the talk checker. Then an agent that did not write the script makes the declaude pass. Then the
diff of the declaude skill finds each claim that the revision lost. Then the talk checker runs again, and
the linter runs once more.

In the passes on the pages of nossaifd, the
declaude pass found problems that `check.py` cannot find. The second run of `check.py` caught what the
revision broke.

The project's author chose on 11 November 2026 to keep each bad example of a deck word for word, as rule 55
of GUIDE.md asks. A talk about writing or about a mistake shows the bad version next to
its fix, and a declaude pass would otherwise rewrite it.

## Choices for documents

The guide for reports makes its own choices. Examples are the passive voice, where the result goes and
short items read in bulk. The page [Why the rules](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd/blob/main/docs/why.md) of
nossaifd gives them.
