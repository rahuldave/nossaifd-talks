# A summary of Schwabish, Better Presentations

This page summarizes each chapter of Jonathan Schwabish's book *Better Presentations*, subtitled "A Guide
for Scholars, Researchers, and Wonks" (Columbia University Press, 2017). The summary is in this guide's own words, and page
numbers are those printed in the book.

Each chapter has a tag. "Words" means the language and the organization of a talk, which is the first
skill. "Look" means visual design, which is the second skill. Where a chapter bears on a choice that the guide had
to make, the summary names the choice by its number. The choices and their answers are in
[the record of the choices](https://github.com/rahuldave/nossaifd-talks/blob/main/docs/notes/schwabish-choices.md),
and [Why the rules](why.md) gives the result.

## The book in one paragraph

A talk is a different form of communication from a paper. Most researchers copy text and figures from the
paper into slides, and so they make the talk for themselves and not for the audience. Schwabish gives
three principles, and every chapter ends with them:

- **Visualize.** Show the content as pictures, because people remember pictures better than words.
- **Unify.** Keep the colors, the fonts, the layout and the spoken words consistent with one another.
- **Focus.** Put little on each slide, and direct the audience's attention to one point at a time.

The book has three parts. Part 1 plans the talk and chooses its look (chapters 1 to 3). Part 2 builds the
slides (chapters 4 to 7). Part 3 gives the talk (chapters 8 and 9). The examples use PowerPoint 2013, but
the advice is for any tool, including Keynote and Beamer.

## Introduction (pages 1 to 7). Words

Researchers follow three phases. They do the research and publish it. Then they turn the paper into
slides in a couple of hours. Then they stand before an audience that has mostly not read the paper. The
book is about the second and third phases.

<u>A talk is a chance to "sell" a result. Schwabish means that the speaker brings people to his side, and</u>
<u>teaches them something that they can use. A reader of the paper has the footnotes and can study a chart.</u>
<u>A listener depends on the speaker's pace and choice of content.</u>

Schwabish cites David Paradi's survey of audiences. The three things that they hate most are a speaker who
reads the slides, full sentences on slides and text that is too small. Clear slides are not about making
things pretty. Researchers love LaTeX for its clean equations, and they should bring the same care to
slides.

He aims at the department seminar, the conference talk, the class lecture and a summary for managers or
funders. Slides are not always needed. A commencement speaker, a keynote or a meeting of four colleagues
can do without them (page 7).

## Chapter 1. Theory, planning and design (pages 11 to 27). Words

<u>**Plan before you open the software.** Outline, write, sketch and draw first.</u>

**The hourglass (pages 11 to 14).** A paper runs like a pyramid. It starts with the introduction and the
literature, goes through the data and the methods, and ends with the result. A talk in that order makes
the audience wait, and it cannot see how the pieces fit.

A news story is an inverted pyramid. It puts the
main fact first, in the "lede", and the details follow in order of importance. But a news story does not
come back to the main fact at the end.

<u>His hourglass joins the two. A preview of the conclusion comes first, and it sets the audience's</u>
<u>expectations. The details and the methods sit in the middle. The end returns to the large theme and to</u>
<u>what the audience should do. Attention is highest at the start and at the end, so the conclusion sits at</u>
<u>both.</u> This bears on choice 2.

<u>Rosalind Reid also draws her story beside an hourglass. Her hourglass is about breadth, wide at the start and the end and narrow in the middle.</u> Schwabish's hourglass is about where the conclusion goes.

<u>**The presentation worksheet (pages 14 to 23).** He fills in a two-page worksheet with ten questions before</u>
<u>he makes any slide</u>:

1. **What kind of talk is it?** A small meeting, a seminar, a lecture, a sales pitch, a keynote or a
   workshop. A keynote needs large pictures and few details. A small meeting can be a discussion, with the speaker as a facilitator.
2. **Who is the audience?** A technical audience wants the method, and a funder wants the result and the
   next step. The level of jargon changes with the audience. A mixed audience is the hardest. Find the
   core of the idea, as Chip and Dan Heath say in *Made to Stick*. To them, "simple" means the core,
   and not "dumbed down". If the level is still unclear, pick the most important group, make a persona of it and
   aim at it. This bears on choice 5.
3. **What is the headline?** One sentence that holds the core idea, short enough to remember and share.
   Carmine Gallo calls it a "Twitter-like" headline. Researchers say that their work has too much nuance.
   Schwabish answers that the abstract already does this job. His example headline is "Technology Can
   Improve Government".
4. **What should the audience do with the conclusions?** Adopt a method, use the data, give money or
   change a policy. The speaker need not say this aloud, but it shapes the talk.
5. **The opening statement.** The first thing said must be bold and full of content. He shows a weak
   opening that thanks the hosts, says the work is unfinished and invites comments. Then he shows a
   strong one that gives the finding, the data and the size of the effect in a few sentences. Do not
   worry that the nuance is missing in the first 30 seconds. This bears on choices 2 and 3.
6. **The closing statement.** Sum up and drive home the message, so that the audience can act on it. He
   shows a weak close that lists the method and the numbers and then thanks the audience. His strong close
   tells the story of one person who applies for food stamps, and then asks the audience to act. Memorize
   the opening and the closing, and do not memorize or read the rest (page 20). This bears on choice 8.
7. **The sections.** <u>At most about five.</u> George Miller says that memory holds about seven items, and later
   work by Jeffrey Rouder says three or four. Name each section by its point, such as "What the research
   has missed" in place of "Previous literature". Sections also let a speaker reuse or cut parts for
   another audience.
8. **The stories.** Stories make anticipation, and people remember them. A story need not be funny or
   personal, but it must support the argument. Examples are the event that started the research, a talk
   with a survey participant or a strange problem with the data. "Your stories will stick with people.
   Your statistics may not."
9. **The visuals.** Plan the graphs, the pictures and the videos. A graph from the paper usually needs
   changes for the screen. <u>Use only the graphs that advance the argument.</u> Write down specific ideas for
   pictures, to avoid stock clichés.
10. **The likely questions and their answers.** Think about the weak points of the work, and what this
    audience will ask. Ask "What questions do you have?" in place of "Do you have any questions?". After
    the last question, restate the closing statement.

**A box, "How we learn" (page 24).** Two theories support his principles. Cognitive load theory, from John
Sweller, says that working memory is small. Richard Mayer's principles of learning say that people learn
more from words and pictures together than from words alone. They also say that matter which does not
serve the lesson hurts learning.

**From outline to design (pages 25 and 26).** Nancy Duarte uses sticky notes. Schwabish uses index cards,
with one message for each slide, coded by color for the sections. Others write an outline in a document,
or draft in the slide tool. Find a process and refine it. We are doing it in an outline form in a markdown file.

## Chapter 2. Color (pages 29 to 50). Look

A good design helps the audience feel positive and learn, as a study by Jan Plass shows. Color can
organize the slides, clarify the data, direct the eye or set a mood. Black and white has high contrast, but
it gives the eye no place to go. One color added to a black-and-white chart, such as one green bar, points
to the finding.

**The palette.** The default colors make every deck look the same. Three or four colors are usually enough,
plus black, white and grey. A palette should hold a light color and a dark color for the text and the
background, and a bright color to highlight. He shows the Urban Institute's palette and three of his own.
He explains the color codes RGB, hex and CMYK, and how to enter them in PowerPoint, Keynote and Beamer.

**A little color theory.** Hue is the name of the color. Value is its lightness, and a tint adds white and a
shade adds black. Saturation is its purity, from rich to grey. Cool colors seem to recede and suit a
background. Warm colors seem to advance and suit the shapes and text in front.

**The background.** Use a light background in a small bright room, because light text on dark can look
washed out there. Use light grey to avoid a blinding white. Use a dark background in a dark or large
room. Avoid busy templates, textures and gradients.

**Contrast.** He describes four kinds of palette. They are one hue in several values, complementary colors,
analogous colors and greys with one bright color. The value contrast matters most for reading, after NASA
Ames.

Avoid red and green of the same value, because about nine percent of men have some color blindness.
A box warns against the rainbow palette for continuous data, because it makes false steps in the data.
Check contrast with tools that test the web accessibility guidelines (WCAG), or print the slide in black
and white.

**Your own palette.** Use tools such as Adobe Color, Color Brewer, Colour Lovers and Design Seeds. Or take
colors from a photo with an eyedropper tool. He made a palette from a photo in about 30 minutes.

## Chapter 3. Type (pages 51 to 61). Look

The look of text forms an impression before anyone reads a word.

**Make the text bigger.** Design for the back row, and make a slide like a billboard. Use 28 points or
more, and 45 or more for a headline with few details. Large type also forces less text. To test, zoom out
to 50 or 60 percent, or stand across the room, or use a real projector.

**Choose a typeface.** A font is serif, sans serif or script. Sans serif is best on a screen, because a
projector can lose the serifs, and studies found it easier to read. Keep serif for very large text or for
print.

Use a family with distinct weights to build a hierarchy. Pair at most two or three fonts, and make
them clearly different. Move away from the defaults, such as Calibri, so that the deck looks less
generic. Check how the numbers in tables and charts look in the font.

**Find new fonts.** He lists Google Fonts, Font Squirrel, MyFonts and Type Genius, and explains how to
install a font. A font that is not on another computer will not show there. So save a PDF, send the fonts
with the deck or embed them.

## Chapter 4. The text slide (pages 65 to 82). Words and look

**Declutter (pages 65 to 70).** <u>Start from a blank slide, and not from the default layout of a title and</u>
<u>bullets. A slide full of text is a crutch for the speaker. Each slide should have one goal.</u>

A slide full of text makes the audience read and stop listening. The cause, after Stanislas Dehaene, is
that reading and listening use the same parts of the brain. The audience also reads faster than the speaker talks,
finishes and drifts away, and so a surprise in the data loses its effect.

If a point is short and the speaker says it, leave it off the slide. Delete the bullet marks, because the
space between lines separates them. Show a logo only on the first and last slides, and leave out the web
address and the slide numbers. A box warns about an orphan, which is a word left alone on the last line,
for example between a number and its unit. The box also asks for no typos, consistent punctuation and no
text in capitals.

**Slide headlines (pages 70 to 73).** <u>Use an active headline that states the point. He prefers "Federal</u>
<u>Health Care Spending Rose Quickly" to "Outlook for the Federal Budget". Build a hierarchy with size and</u>
<u>weight, so that the most important point is largest.</u>

Avoid shadows, glow and 3D text. When a long quote
must be shown, stand silent and let the audience read it. <u>Then highlight the part that matters.</u> This
bears on choice 6.

**Layering (pages 73 to 77).** <u>Split a full slide into several slides, one point each, with the current</u>
<u>point dark and the earlier points grey.</u> Build the last slide first, and then remove or recolor text for
the earlier ones. Animation can do the same, with only "Appear" and "Disappear". Slides cost nothing, and he rejects the rule of one slide for each minute.

<u>Do not show all the text in light grey and darken it point by</u>
<u>point, because the audience still reads ahead.</u> This bears on choice 7.

**A box, "The previous literature slide" (page 78).** Do not list every author and date from the paper.
Show the two or three works that matter to the point, and layer them by group.

**Layering equations (pages 78 to 80).** <u>Walk through an equation term by term, or show it whole and then</u>
<u>explain each term in color.</u> Show a simple version, and describe the full one. Describe the notation, even
symbols that the audience should know.

**From text to visuals (pages 80 to 82).** <u>Turn a sentence that holds numbers into a chart under an active headline.</u> <u>Show the change and not the levels when the change is the point.</u>

The chapter is mostly about words, such as one goal for each slide, less text, headlines, layering, equations and the
literature. The grey of layering, the hierarchy of type and the highlights are the look.

## Chapter 5. The data visualization slide (pages 83 to 115). Look

This chapter is about charts on slides. The project's author chose to keep chart advice for the separate
visualization repository, and said that both books are for the look of slides. So the slide parts of the
chapter go to the skill on visual design.

**Preattentive attributes (pages 84 to 87).** The eye notices some features before conscious attention, such as
color, intensity, size, shape, position, enclosure and others. Use one or two of them to point at the
finding, and turn the rest grey. Too many at once compete.

Show results as charts and not as bullets or
tables. Put the legend on the chart. Remove heavy gridlines, extra ticks, shading and 3D.

**Layering graphs (pages 88 to 96).** <u>Split a dense chart into a series of slides that add one series or</u>
<u>category at a time. Grey out the earlier ones. His example is a chart of college costs from the College</u>
<u>Board, remade as five slides.</u>

Lock the axes so that nothing shifts between slides. Show the empty axes
first, then the data. Use a horizontal bar chart in place of labels turned 90 degrees. Move long sources
and notes to an "endnote" slide at the end.

**Redesigns (pages 97 to 108).** A "spaghetti" line chart becomes one highlighted line at a time, or small
multiples. A "clutterplot" scatter from Stata highlights the five countries in the story, with full names
and an active title. Pie charts are hard to compare, so use bars, paired columns, stacked bars or a slope
chart. Never use 3D charts, because the third dimension distorts the values.

**Tables (pages 108 to 113).** A 40-row regression table does not work on a screen, and layering it does
not help. Show only the numbers that make the point, round them, align the decimals and mark significance
with color. Better still, turn the table into a chart.

**Inserting graphs (pages 113 to 115).** Paste charts from Excel as high-quality images or as live
objects, and know the cost of each. Keep the charts the same size and place across layered slides. Draw
a very simple chart from shapes.

## Chapter 6. The image slide (pages 117 to 133). Look

<u>Pictures with speech help people remember. Alan Paivio found that people recall a topic twice as often</u>
<u>with a picture. Pictures can also move the audience's feelings. A picture must support the content and</u>
<u>not decorate it.</u>

**Full-screen images (pages 118 to 124).** <u>Make a picture fill the slide, and put large text on its light</u>
<u>part, or on a box with some transparency.</u> <u>Arrange several pictures to fill the slide together.</u>

Place a person so that he or she looks toward the text. Use the rule of thirds, which puts the subject off center.
Choose photos with empty space, blur or gradients for the text. Avoid small, stretched, watermarked or
cliché stock photos.

**Finding images (pages 124 to 128).** Search for a specific scene, such as "coastal damages from
Hurricane Sandy", in place of a vague word. He lists sites for photos, such as Unsplash, Pexels and
Wikimedia Commons, and for icons, such as the Noun Project. He does not use Google Images. Icons can mark
the topics of a talk and come back on later slides. Designers can make custom pictures.

**Copyright and attribution (pages 128 and 129).** <u>Credit a picture as you would cite research.</u> He explains
the Creative Commons licenses. Ask the creator when the license is unclear. A license for a talk may not
cover a book or a website. Put a short credit in the lower right of the slide, and the long ones on the
endnote slide.

**Large images and videos (pages 129 to 133).** Download the largest version, because a stretched picture
blurs. Keep each deck's pictures in its own folder, with their sources. Use a video only when it supports
the message. Download it, trim it, put it in the deck, play it full screen, and keep its file next to the
deck.

## Chapter 7. The scaffolding slides (pages 135 to 143). Words and look

<u>Scaffolding slides carry no content. They guide the audience from part to part.</u>

**Title slide (pages 135 and 136).** <u>It is on screen while people sit down, so make it set the tone.</u> Cut
the date, the address and the phone number. Keep the email. <u>Add a picture that fits the content.</u>

**Agenda slide (pages 136 to 138).** A list of what the speaker will do serves the speaker. Say it instead,
or skip it and start. A better agenda is a timeline, with each section's width set by its time, after
Garr Reynolds. Another is a set of icons that come back later. A timeline helps most for a full day with
breaks.

**Header slides (pages 139 and 140).** <u>A header slide marks the start of each section, with its title and
perhaps a picture or a large number.</u> Do not linger on it, and say the transition aloud. The slides after it
can drop the section title.

<u>Attention falls after about 10 minutes, after research that John Medina cites.</u>
<u>Medina uses a "hook" every 10 minutes, such as a story, a case or an exercise, which must be relevant to</u>
<u>the content.</u> A video, a story or a break for questions can also mark a section.

**Breaker slides (pages 140 and 141).** A blank slide in the middle of the talk wakes the audience and turns
it back to the speaker. The keys B and W in PowerPoint blank the screen.

**Ending slides (pages 141 and 142).** <u>A last slide that says "Thank You!" or "Questions?" wastes the</u>
<u>moment. It also signals the end, and people start to pack up.</u>

<u>Say the thanks and ask "What questions do you</u>
<u>have?" aloud. Put the headline on the last slide, alone or with a picture.</u> A small group can get a list of
next steps, and the contact details can go there too. This bears on choice 4.

**A different view (pages 142 and 143).** Use the slide sorter, or the light table in Keynote, to see the
whole deck. Check that the sections hang together and that the look is consistent.

The words are what each slide is for and what the speaker says. The look of title, header and breaker
slides goes to the skill on visual design.

## Chapter 8. Presenting (pages 147 to 155). Words

**Practice (pages 147 to 150).** Practice aloud, standing, with the clicker, and do not stop after the
first runs. A 15-minute talk can be practiced four times in an hour. Practice shows the parts that do not
flow, and it teaches how to present the content, which differs from knowing it.

Practice also shows the
length. Plan for 70 to 80 percent of the time, which leaves time for questions and for a session that runs
late. Ending 10 minutes early pleases everyone. He prefers to speak last in a session, so that his last
slide stays up during the discussion.

**Record yourself (pages 149 and 150).** A recording shows fillers, such as "um", "uh", "so" and "like", and
verbal tics, such as "strong" for every number or "the thing is" before every point. It shows jargon that
does not suit the audience. It also shows speed.

People recall less from fast speech, after Michael
Gordon. Slow down by breathing and pausing. Speed up and get louder to drive a point home, and slow down
for an equation. A pause gives the audience time to absorb, and silence can build tension.

**Stories (page 150).** A colleague presented the data on poor families in the usual academic way. Her
team's stories about the families were far stronger, and the advice was to start with them. Stories make
an emotional link. Keep them short and on the subject, and they can come from anywhere in the speaker's
life.

**Delivery and nerves (pages 151 and 152).** Nerves are normal. Calm them with practice, stretching and deep
breaths, arriving early, exercise, and picturing the talk going well. James Driskell's review of 35
studies supports that picturing. Do not apologize at the start for the projector, the room or unfinished
work, because it sets a tone of doubt. Talk with some listeners before the talk, which calms the speaker
and gives friendly faces.

**Emotions matter (pages 152 to 155).** The audience picks up the speaker's emotions. Be confident and
enthusiastic, even for the hundredth time. Stand in the center or beside the screen, and not behind the
podium. Keep good posture, set up the microphone, smile, use small gestures and walk with purpose.

Walk
to the screen to point. Dress a little more formally than the audience. Make eye contact with each person.
Get the audience to do something, such as raise hands, answer or post on social media.

## Chapter 9. The technical nitty-gritty (pages 157 to 168). Words and look

**Notes (pages 157 and 158).** <u>He would rather use notes than forget a point. He prefers the notes pane
and the presenter view, which shows the next slide and a timer</u>. Keep a printed copy in case the computer is
far away. <u>Keep the notes short, as bullet points</u>, and do not read them word for word, because reading
sounds stiff and breaks eye contact. This bears on choice 8.

**Aspect ratio (pages 159 to 161).** <u>Most screens are now 16:9, so make slides 16:9.</u> A mismatch adds black
bars, so accept it and do not edit the slides just before the talk. Converting a deck can stretch the
pictures.

**Setup and tools (pages 161 to 164).** He lists a kit for the bag. It holds adapters for the projector, extension
cords, a clicker, batteries, a USB drive with the deck as PowerPoint and PDF, tape, and markers or chalk.
Arrive early to test. Turn off the internet and notifications, quit other programs, stop the computer from
sleeping and click through the deck. Learn the culture of the audience, such as whether people interrupt
and when they arrive.

**Online talks (pages 164 and 165).** <u>An online audience is easily distracted, and the speaker cannot see
it. Slides can carry a little more text there. Tell the audience when you pause for them to read. Use
exercises, questions and the chat</u>. Test the slides in the webinar tool, and get a good microphone.

**Handouts and sharing (pages 165 to 168).** A printed copy of the whole deck draws the audience away from
the speaker. Give a handout only when it is needed, and say when to look at it. Keep it to a table or a
page of equations.

Put detail for the likely questions in appendix slides after the last slide, and link
to them. A deck made for speaking does not work for reading, and no single deck can do both. The choices
are two decks, a companion document, notes written for a reader, or a recording of the talk.

## Conclusions (pages 169 and 170). Words

Do not start at the computer. Think about the message and what the audience should do. Visualize the
content, unify what you say with what you show, and focus the audience on one point at a time. A talk
gives the audience the speaker, and a paper cannot.

## Further readings (pages 171 and 172)

He lists books, blogs and tools on presentations and design. In the text he also cites Garr Reynolds and
Nancy Duarte. A box in chapter 5 lists books on charts, for example by Edward Tufte, Stephen Few, Alberto
Cairo and Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic.
